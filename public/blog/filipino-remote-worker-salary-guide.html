Filipino Remote Worker Salary Guide 2026: What to Pay (By Role)

By WorkBase PH · Updated June 2026 · 11 min read

One of the most common questions employers ask before hiring from the Philippines is: "What should I actually be paying?" Pay too little and you'll lose good candidates to better-paying employers. Pay what the market expects and you'll attract talent that stays.

This guide covers real 2026 salary benchmarks for the most common remote roles hired from the Philippines, along with a cost comparison that shows exactly how much you save versus hiring locally.

Note on currency: All rates below are in USD per month for full-time (40 hrs/week) work unless stated otherwise. Part-time rates are roughly 50–60% of the full-time equivalent, not 50%, because part-time roles often command a slight premium per hour.

What's Not Included (and What Is)

When you hire a Filipino remote worker as an independent contractor (the most common arrangement), your cost is generally the agreed salary — nothing more. Unlike local hires, you are typically not responsible for:

  • Employer payroll taxes or social security contributions
  • Health insurance premiums
  • Office space or equipment (most Filipino workers have their own setup)
  • Paid leave entitlements (though paying a 13th-month bonus is the norm and builds strong loyalty)

Filipino workers are responsible for their own tax filings with the BIR (Bureau of Internal Revenue) as independent contractors. You should use a simple service contract to formalise the engagement.

The 13th Month Bonus

The 13th month bonus is a deeply embedded norm in Philippine work culture. It's legally required for regular employees under Philippine law, and while it doesn't legally apply to independent contractors working for foreign employers, most Filipino remote workers expect it as a sign of respect and loyalty.

It's simply one extra month's salary paid in December (or split across mid-year and December). For a $700/month worker, that's an additional $700/year — a small cost that dramatically increases retention and loyalty.

Should You Pay in USD or PHP?

Most Filipino remote workers prefer to be paid in USD because it protects them from peso fluctuations and is easier to receive via Wise, Payoneer, or PayPal. USD rates are also the market standard on international hiring platforms.

You can agree on either, but quoting in USD simplifies negotiation and avoids awkward conversations when the exchange rate shifts.

The Bottom Line on Pay

Don't try to pay the absolute minimum. The Filipino remote worker market is competitive, and the best candidates have options. A mid-level VA earning $500/month receives dozens of job offers. If you're offering $350 for someone with 3+ years of experience, you'll attract junior candidates or lose your hire quickly.

Pay the mid-range for the role, add a 13th-month bonus, pay on time every time, and you'll have a loyal, productive team member who isn't quietly interviewing elsewhere.

Part-Time vs Full-Time: What's the Right Rate?

Part-time rates are not simply half of a full-time monthly rate. Filipino workers typically charge a slightly higher hourly rate for part-time arrangements because of the uncertainty and the flexibility being provided. Here's how to think about it:

How to Handle Salary Reviews and Raises

Filipino workers rarely ask for raises — even when they deserve one. It's cultural. They're unlikely to negotiate assertively, which can lead to them quietly accepting a lower rate while job searching on the side.

The best employers build in proactive salary reviews:

  • 6-month review: After the probationary period, assess performance and adjust if the person has exceeded expectations. Even a $50–$100/month increase signals that you notice and value their work.
  • Annual review: Benchmark against current market rates. If the market has moved and your rate hasn't, your best people will leave — quietly and without warning.
  • Performance bonuses: A one-time bonus for outstanding work on a specific project is a low-cost, high-impact retention tool. Even $50–$200 is meaningful.

The retention math: Replacing a skilled Filipino VA typically costs 1–3 months of salary in lost productivity, rehiring time, and onboarding. A proactive $100/month raise to retain them costs $1,200/year — far less than the cost of turnover.

What "Mid-Level" Actually Means in This Context

The salary table above uses entry, mid, and senior tiers. Here's what those mean in practice for the most common roles:

General VA

  • Entry: 0–2 years, familiar with basic tools (Google Workspace, email), limited experience with international clients
  • Mid: 2–4 years, experienced with Western business norms, proficient in 3–5 specific tools, has worked remotely for at least one long-term employer
  • Senior: 4+ years, can manage workflows independently, may supervise other VAs, familiar with project management, strong communication skills

Bookkeeper / Accounting VA

  • Entry: Accounting graduate, familiar with QuickBooks or Xero at a basic level, limited exposure to international tax rules
  • Mid: 2–4 years of remote bookkeeping for US/AU/UK clients, proficient in one or more major platforms, handles reconciliation independently
  • Senior/CPA: Licensed CPA or equivalent experience, handles complex accounts, familiar with GST/BAS/sales tax, can liaise with local accountants on behalf of the employer

Frequently Asked Questions

Should I offer a salary or hourly rate?

For full-time and long-term part-time arrangements, a monthly salary is strongly preferred by Filipino workers. It provides financial predictability. Hourly rates work for project-based or very minimal commitments (under 10 hours/week), but for anyone you want to retain long-term, a fixed monthly rate signals commitment on your end.

Do I have to pay Philippine taxes on behalf of my VA?

No. When hiring as an independent contractor, the Filipino worker is responsible for their own tax filings with the BIR. You are not required to withhold or remit taxes on their behalf. Document the arrangement with a simple service contract to be clear on the independent contractor status.

Is there a minimum wage I need to follow?

Philippine minimum wage laws apply to locally-registered employers. As a foreign employer hiring a Filipino contractor remotely, you are not legally bound by Philippine minimum wage rules. That said, paying below market rates is both ethically questionable and practically counterproductive — you'll attract only the weakest candidates and churn through hires quickly.

Can I negotiate below the rate ranges listed?

You can try, but for roles at the mid and senior level, undercutting the market by more than 10–15% will cost you candidates. The best Filipino workers have multiple offers. They'll take the employer who pays fairly and treats them well — not necessarily the highest, but not below the floor either.

What's a reasonable probationary period?

Most employers use a 30–90 day probationary period where either party can end the arrangement with shorter notice (7–14 days). After probation, standard contract notice periods apply. Some employers also offer a slightly reduced rate during probation, with an automatic increase after 30–60 days of good performance — this is well-received and sets a positive trajectory.
