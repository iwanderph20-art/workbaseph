Freelancer Safety: How to Spot and Avoid Job Scams

By WorkBase PH · September 2026 · 6 min read

Most employers on WorkBase PH are exactly who they say they are. But remote work also attracts scammers who impersonate employers to get money or personal information out of job seekers. Knowing the patterns below takes less than five minutes and can save you from a costly mistake.

The golden rule: a real job pays you. You should never have to pay, wire, refund, or "process a fee" to an employer at any stage of hiring.

1. Being Asked to Pay for Anything Upfront

This is the single most common freelance job scam. It shows up as a "training fee," a "starter kit" or equipment fee, a background-check charge, a software license, or a refundable "deposit" you're told you'll get back. Sometimes it's framed as reimbursing a shipping cost for a laptop or supplies that were supposedly sent to you.

  • Real employers absorb the cost of onboarding, tools, and equipment themselves.
  • No legitimate company will ask a candidate to wire money, load GCash/PayMaya credits, or buy gift cards before work begins.
  • If a "refund" is promised later, that's the scam — the money never comes back.

2. Being Rushed Off-Platform to Telegram, WhatsApp, or Signal

Scammers push conversations off the hiring platform fast — sometimes within the first message — because it removes any record of what was said and any way for a platform's team to step in if something goes wrong. Watch for:

  • An "interview" that happens entirely over chat, with no video call and no verifiable company details.
  • Being added to a Telegram or WhatsApp group full of other "new hires" who all seem enthusiastic and are all typing at once.
  • Vague answers when you ask which company you'd actually be working for.

Keep the paper trail. Legitimate interviews and offers on WorkBase PH happen through the platform's built-in messaging and interview scheduler for a reason — it protects both sides.

3. Overpayment and Fake Check Scams

An "employer" sends a check or bank transfer for more than your agreed pay and asks you to send the difference back, often to buy equipment "on their behalf." The original payment later bounces or is reversed, and you're left having sent real money to a stranger.

4. Requests for Sensitive Information Before a Real Interview

Be cautious about sharing government ID scans, full bank account details, online banking credentials, or one-time passwords (OTPs) before you've had a genuine interview and a clear, written scope of work. No legitimate employer needs your OTP or online banking password — ever, for any reason.

5. Too Good to Be True Pay for Minimal Work

High pay for "just a few clicks a day," data entry that pays hundreds of dollars for an hour of work, or a job that never actually explains what the work is — these are classic bait. If the pay-to-effort ratio doesn't make business sense, it usually isn't real.

6. Pressure and Urgency Tactics

"Reply in the next 10 minutes or we give the slot to someone else." Real hiring processes can move quickly, but they don't rely on artificial deadlines to stop you from thinking it through, asking questions, or checking the employer out.

Quick Red-Flag Checklist

  • Any request for payment, however small or "refundable"
  • Pushed to Telegram/WhatsApp before a real interview
  • Vague company name, no verifiable details, generic email domain
  • Pay that's clearly disproportionate to the work described
  • Pressure to decide or pay "right now"
  • Asked for OTPs, banking passwords, or ID scans with no clear reason

What To Do If You Spot One

Stop responding, don't send any money or personal information, and report it. On WorkBase PH:

  1. Go to the Jobs tab in your dashboard.
  2. Use the Report a concern link in the advisory banner at the top.
  3. Paste in the job posting link (the short link shown on the job card, e.g. workbaseph.com/jobs/CODE) and describe what happened.

Our team reviews every report. Flagging a suspicious post — even if you're not sure — helps protect every other freelancer who might see it next.
